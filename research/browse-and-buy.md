# Browse and buy: research for Brittany's shop

## Top recommendations for Brittany's site

1. **Put the shop on the first screen.** Keep the turning portrait, add one line ("Handmade glass-bead necklaces, each one of a kind") and a visible "Shop all" button. Start the product grid right below it. Catbird, Lizzie Fortunato and Bea the Beadmaker all reach products within a scroll or two.
2. **Keep quick view, but make it a real shortcut.** Baymard now recommends quick view for visual products. Dim the page behind it, show a clear "See full details" link without scrolling, and make Back close the popup and return to the grid. Also give each necklace its own page URL for sharing and search.
3. **Put the buying info inside quick view.** At least 3 photos, price, length, bead material, and a one-line shipping and returns note. Baymard says shoppers look for return info on the product page, and 44% of sites omit it.
4. **Add a worn-on-the-neck photo for every necklace**, plus a flat close-up of the beads and clasp. Baymard found 37% of sites lack "in scale" images and 23% lack model images for wearables.
5. **Keep grid photos consistent** (same background, light, framing) so pieces compare easily, and show the on-body photo on hover or swipe (NN/g).
6. **Add a color filter** (small multi-select color dots) and an Available / Sold toggle. Baymard calls color an essential filter. With about 7 pieces, skip price and type filters.
7. **Say "1 of 1" once, plainly:** a small tag on each card and in quick view, "One of one. When it's gone, it's gone." No countdown timers or "only 2 left" banners. The FTC lists fake scarcity and timers as deceptive design.
8. **Keep sold pieces in a "Found a home" section** below the available ones, labeled "Sold" with the month, with a "Want something like this? Tell me." form. Mociun, WWAKE, Art in Motion, Vintage Jewelry and Bea's shop all keep sold work. It proves pieces sell and generates custom requests.
9. **Prevent double-selling.** Stock of 1, no backorders, and the Buy button disappears the moment a piece sells (Barn2's WooCommerce method). If a cart holds a piece, say how long.
10. **Make the email list about drops, not discounts.** Name it ("Notes from the studio" is Bea's). Promise what you will send: first look at new necklaces, occasional studio notes. Add "Tell me when the next batch is ready" on sold cards. Skip the 10%-off pop-up that Catbird and Wolf Circus use.
11. **Make buying low-effort.** Guest checkout first (19% abandon when forced to make an account). Show full price, shipping, and a delivery date range before the last step. Add a sticky bottom Buy button on phones. A slide-out cart is fine for 1-3 items.
12. **Write the maker story in Brittany's voice, below the grid:** first person, real details (how she started, the torch, the glass), a photo of her at work, a "Materials and care" note, and shipping and returns in the footer.

## How strong shops are laid out (all opened)

- **Catbird:** announcements, nav, hero, category and price tiles, feature tiles, reviews, values, email signup late, footer with returns, sizing and care.
- **Mejuri:** short hero, two category cards, brand story, stores. No email form.
- **Lizzie Fortunato:** hero, collections, product carousel with price and Add to cart, founder story, Instagram, footer. Free-shipping threshold in top bar.
- **Wolf Circus:** nav by product type, "new" hero, carousels, category tiles, email signup. No story on the homepage.
- **Bea the Beadmaker (indie lampwork):** shipping note, maker intro, process, care tips, product grid, review badge, newsletter. The closest model for Brittany.

Pattern: hero, products within one or two scrolls, a story block, trust links in the footer. The big brands lean on discounts and stores, which Brittany doesn't need. She needs the story and trust lines closer to the products.

## Browsing

- **Grid (NN/g):** photos should be large, consistent, show alternate views on hover, and show the product worn or in use. Mignon Faget's close-ups alone left a tester wanting more.
- **Color (Baymard):** show all color options in the list; 57% of sites don't.
- **Quick view (Baymard):** good for visual products, bad for spec-heavy ones. On mobile, people mistook it for the full page. A button in the middle of the photo can cause accidental opens (secondary source). A centered overlay beat a side panel. It complements a good grid; it doesn't replace one.

## Buying

- **Product page (NN/g):** name, large zoomable images, full price, availability, clear add-to-cart, concise description with key facts first. Multiple views and in-use shots matter.
- **Jewelry specifics (my inference, not from NN/g):** length in inches, bead size, materials, clasp, weight, care.
- **Costs (Baymard):** top reasons for abandoning are extra costs (39%), slow delivery (21%), forced account (19%), complex checkout (18%). Show the total near the buy button.
- **Cart drawer vs page:** weak evidence (vendor blog, no head-to-head test). It leans toward a full cart page for considered purchases like fine jewelry. For tiny carts, either works.
- **Mobile:** sticky bottom buy button, swipeable photos, roughly 48px tap targets (Inchoo; practitioner advice, not research).
- **Reviews:** with one-off pieces, use short customer notes or photos of the necklace worn (with permission) rather than star ratings.

## One-of-a-kind selling

- **Sold pieces:** Mociun's archive lists 1,095 "Sold out" items. Vintage Jewelry badges "Sold [month]" and says the archive helps buyers "point at one." WWAKE shows "Sold Out - Inquire." Art in Motion says a piece "will never exist again" and invites commissions.
- **Gap to beat:** Art in Motion and WWAKE offered no notify or "like this" form on sold cards; Art in Motion had one inconsistent card. A simple form on each sold card would be better.
- **Honest scarcity:** each piece really is made once. Say that, and stop there.
- **Drops:** Vintage Jewelry and Bea invite visitors to a list for new work. A first look for subscribers before the public shop update rewards signing up (my suggestion).

## Generic vs personal

- **Generic tells** (one unopened design blog snippet and one opened vendor page; practitioner opinion, not research): stock-style images, headlines any business could use, icon-and-one-liner cards, invented-sounding testimonials, default palettes, no concrete business detail.
- **Personal:** Larissa Spafford's site tells her story in first person with humor and family detail, with a friendly mailing-list note. Bea explains her process and who her buyers are.
- **For Brittany:** her own photos, real piece names, how long each took, what inspired it. Test each sentence: could any shop say this?

Limits: Baymard's paid guidelines weren't available, and I found no independent research on how visitors perceive AI-made sites.
